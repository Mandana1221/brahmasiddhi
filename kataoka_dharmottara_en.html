Kataoka Kei on Dharmottara's Theory of Apoha (A Summary of His Published Papers)

Since around 2010, Kataoka Kei has reread the apoha theory of Dharmottara (c. 740–800) through the eyes of his Brahmin contemporaries and critics (Jayanta, Sucarita, Vācaspati), rather than only from inside the Buddhist tradition. This page summarizes, with figures and tables, the claims made in eight of his papers (2012h, 2013c, 2014a, 2014e, 2014f, 2016a, 2017e, 2018d).

For Dharmottara, the object of conceptual cognition and of words is neither a form within cognition nor an external real. It is something fabricated (āropita): presented as existing although it does not exist. On this one point Dharmottara departs decisively from his master Dharmakīrti.

1. Three models of apoha2010c · 2012h · 2014e · 2014f

The Buddhist apoha theory developed in three stages: external, internal, and neither internal nor external. Dharmottara represents the third stage and takes a path different from Dharmakīrti's.

In his Nyāyamañjarī, the ninth-century Naiyāyika Jayanta presents the Buddhist theory of apoha in three forms. Kataoka uses this division as the framework for the history of the theory.

Fig. 1. Kataoka's arrangement, based on Jayanta's threefold division. Jayanta glosses the phrase "neither cognition nor external" (बुद्धिर्नो न बहिः) of AP's opening verse as "neither internal nor external" (नान्तर्न बहिः).

2. āropita: fabricated, not superimposed2012h · 2014f · 2017e

Dharmottara's key term āropita does not mean "A placed upon B" (superimposed). It means "presented as existing although it does not exist" (fabricated).

The words samāropa and āropa have two meanings. Which one we choose changes the whole picture of Dharmottara's theory.

Fig. 2. Two kinds of āropa, after Kataoka 2017e §2. Ratnakīrti distinguishes the former as "grasping X in what is not X" (atasmiṃs tadgrahaḥ) and the latter as "appearance of the non-existent" (asatkhyāti).

3. All four readings of adhyavasāya rejected2013c · 2014f · 2017e

Dharmottara lists four ways of understanding Dharmakīrti's "determination" (adhyavasāya) and rejects all of them, superimposition included. Akamatsu (1984) misread one of them as Dharmottara's own view.

In AP §3.2 an opponent offers four readings of what it means for conceptual cognition to "determine" its own form as external. Dharmottara accepts none of them.

4. The real target is Dharmakīrti2014e · 2014f

What Dharmottara rejects is Dharmakīrti's own view that a cognitive form is the object of conceptual cognition. Since he cannot criticize his master openly, he criticizes him implicitly and reinterprets the passages that contradict him.

5. Sameness is imposed from above2014e

What makes distinct particulars "the same" is not that they produce the same effect, but that they share one fabricated object. Dharmottara revives Dignāga's top-down model without positing external reals.

Fig. 5. How sameness arises. The decisive sentence is AP §9.8: "not because the effect is the same, but because the object is the same." Sucarita (Kāśikā 3.3.4) and Vācaspati (Nyāyakaṇikā) restate it in the same order.
DharmakīrtiDharmottara
What makes particulars the sameProducing the same effect (judgment) (ekārthasādhana)Having the same object (one fabricated thing)
Direct object of conceptual cognitionA cognitive form (different in each cognition)The fabricated (one and shared)
Type of modelBottom-upTop-down (close to Dignāga)

In Dharmakīrti's model, different judgments count as "one" because they have the same form (image). But a form is not other than its cognition, so each is a distinct particular (svalakṣaṇa), and distinct particulars cannot be "one." Kataoka reads an implicit criticism of Dharmakīrti here.

Source: Kataoka 2014e §4, §7.

6. Brahmin authors saw two Buddhist theories2014a · 2016a · 2018d

Brahmin authors of the ninth and tenth centuries divided the Buddhist apoha theory into a cognitive-form theory and Dharmottara's fabrication theory, and criticized them separately. Treating Dharmottara's view as a distinct theory is not Kataoka's invention; it is how his contemporaries saw it.

7. Recovering the lost Sanskrit2014a · 2016a · 2017e · 2018d

AP survives only in Tibetan translation. Because Sucarita's Kāśikā and other works follow its arguments closely, parts of the Sanskrit can be reconstructed.

Kataoka has edited the previously unpublished apoha section of Sucarita's Kāśikā from four manuscripts (Kataoka 2014a, 2015a, 2019b). In it Sucarita closely follows the structure of Dharmottara's text.

Fig. 7. Sources for reconstructing AP's Sanskrit. Kataoka's reconstructions are given in Kataoka 2017e and 2018d.

8. Place in intellectual history2012h · 2013c · 2014f · 2018d

Dharmottara returned apoha to "mere non-existence." This is a return to Dignāga, and it parallels the alīkākāravāda line in Yogācāra theories of mental forms.

Later developments

  • Dharmottara's followers seem to have developed a revised version in which "the externality of the fabricated" (āropitabāhyatva, alīkabāhyatva) is the object of words. The term does not yet appear in AP; it appears in Prajñākaragupta, Vācaspati, Jñānaśrīmitra and Śrīdhara (Kataoka 2014a, 2018d).
  • Jñānaśrīmitra and Ratnakīrti, who accept cognitive forms, criticized Dharmottara as a "negativist" (pratiṣedhavādin) (Kataoka 2017e, conclusion 4).
  • Kataoka argues that the Ratnakīrti-based view of the history (Akamatsu's view) cannot explain how Jayanta and Sucarita saw things. He proposes that apoha theory after Dharmottara be viewed within the larger frame of an opposition between the cognitive-form theory and the fabrication theory (Kataoka 2014f, conclusions 5–6).

Sources: Kataoka 2012h; Kataoka 2013c §7; Kataoka 2014f, conclusions 5–6; Kataoka 2014a, 2018d.
