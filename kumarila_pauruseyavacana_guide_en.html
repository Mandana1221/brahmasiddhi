INDIAN PHILOSOPHY 101 — READING A PAPER THROUGH DIAGRAMS

Can We Trust Human Words?
Kumārila on Pauruṣeyavacana

Paper discussed: Kei Kataoka, "Kumārila's Notion of Pauruṣeyavacana," Rivista di Studi Sudasiatici II (2007): 39–55.

★ The paper in a nutshell

In the Mīmāṃsā school, man-made statements can convey only what is in the speaker's mind (his cognition), not the external object itself. Kumārila inherited this idea from Śabara and refined it: he accepted human speech as one kind of "means of valid cognition," but limited its domain to the speaker's cognition. The author argues that the Buddhist Dharmakīrti's well-known claim that "speech merely indicates the speaker's intention" was prefigured not so much by Dignāga as by this Mīmāṃsā discussion.

2The traditional picture: primary and secondary sources

In Mīmāṃsā, human speech is not ultimately counted as a primary source of information. In mundane and religious matters alike, it is a secondary source that depends on something else.

Table A. In every domain, human speech comes second

Mundane matters (the visible world)Religious matters (dharma)
① Primary sourcePerception, etc.Vedic injunction
↓ based on this↓ based on this
② Secondary sourceHuman speechHuman speech (including Manu's teaching)

So human speech is valid only if it is based on perception or the like (or on the Veda), and is also conveyed correctly by an honest speaker.

Table B. The big divide between "direct" and "indirect"

Mīmāṃsā habitually sorts things into "direct" (sākṣāt) and "indirect" (dvāreṇa, by way of something else). The relation between perception and inference maps neatly onto that between the Veda and smṛti, and onto that between an original source and human speech.

DirectPerceptionVedic injunctionOriginal source (mūla)
IndirectInferenceSmṛti (tradition)Human speech
An analogy: the same structure as memory

When you remember what you had for dinner yesterday, the memory is correct only because you actually experienced eating it. Memory alone is not an independent ground.
Human speech works the same way: it is correct only when it rests on what the speaker actually saw (perceived). Kumārila, presenting an opponent's position, puts it this way: speech conveys what is known through another means of cognition, and like memory it cannot be valid by itself (Ślokavārttika, Codanā chapter, v. 22).

Why two views?

Because the two are joined by "furthermore," the author suggests that view ① was inherited from the earlier tradition, while view ② was added as something new, by Śabara himself or by someone else. View ② aims to bring out the difference from the Veda, so it should not be over-emphasized. Still, it is a historical fact that Śabara's commentator Kumārila developed this idea further.

Since Mīmāṃsā is often said to be optimistic about language and the world, this skeptical view may come as a surprise.

4Kumārila (1): human speech conveys only the speaker's cognition

Kumārila (ca. 600–650) develops Śabara's view ② in detail in the Codanā chapter of his Ślokavārttika, especially vv. 160–168.

Key point: even a trustworthy person's words reach only the speaker's cognition

Śabara simply distinguished true and false statements, without paying much attention to liars or confused speakers. Kumārila sets liars and confused speakers aside in advance as exceptions and narrows the discussion to the normal case of a trustworthy speaker. Then he says:

From a trustworthy person's sentence, the speaker's cognition is understood; in other cases, it may go astray. Ślokavārttika, Codanā chapter, v. 162ab (free rendering)

The hearer does understand the object from the statement. Even so, that understanding is valid only regarding the speaker's cognition. To be sure about the object itself, there must be a proper cause (perception, the Veda, etc.) behind the speaker's cognition.

Elsewhere (v. 176cd), Kumārila interprets Śabara's talk of a sentence operating on an external object as merely provisional (abhyupetya). The author therefore concludes that Kumārila's own final view is this negative one: human speech operates on the speaker's cognition.

Why did Kumārila shift his position? A changing era

The author sees a paradigm change in Kumārila's time. For example, the Naiyāyika Pakṣilasvāmin (Nyāyabhāṣya 1.1.7) states that the definition of verbal testimony is common to sages, Āryas and mlecchas ("barbarians"). Even a mleccha can be "trustworthy," and his words can be valid. This can be read as a criticism of the conservative Mīmāṃsā view.
Amid this secular common sense, Kumārila could no longer hold on to the old view and reached a compromise, the author suggests.

6Resolving the tension: separate "domains"

Kumārila was caught in a dilemma.

The demand of his era

He had to accept that human speech is valid.

The tradition of his school

Giving human speech unlimited power would raise it to the same rank as the Veda. That could not be allowed.

Śabara's view ② gave him the hint. Kumārila's solution: "Human speech is a pramāṇa, but its domain is limited to the speaker's cognition." Each means of valid cognition has its own clearly demarcated territory.

★ The single most important point of the paper

For Kumārila, human speech is accepted as a subcategory of pramāṇa. But it is valid within its own domain (the speaker's cognition), not directly valid about the external object. He kept Śabara's idea that "it conveys only the speaker's cognition," but recast it as "a pramāṇa, though with a limited range."

How does Manu's teaching become valid "about the object"?

Manu's words, too, are at first valid only about Manu's cognition. But when we ask what the source of Manu's teaching is, there are five candidates. Kumārila picks the least complicated explanation by means of presumption (arthāpatti) (Tantravārttika on 1.3.2).

7In the history of ideas: the link to Dharmakīrti

The Buddhist logician Dharmakīrti is famous for claiming that words have no necessary connection with things, so external objects cannot be established from words: words merely indicate the speaker's intention (vaktrabhiprāya-sūcaka) (Pramāṇavārttika-svavṛtti). This is usually thought to develop the apoha theory of his fellow Buddhist Dignāga. The author questions this.

★ The author's conclusion

The essential part of Dharmakīrti's idea that "speech indicates only the speaker's intention" is found not in Dignāga's works but already in Śabara's commentary. If Dharmakīrti presupposes Kumārila (or a similar Mīmāṃsā view), it is more appropriate to say that the idea was prefigured by Kumārila or other Mīmāṃsakas. Seeing it as shaped by the earlier Buddhist tradition alone is unhistorical.

Supporting evidence: Dharmakīrti's earliest work was composed mainly against the Mīmāṃsā, and some of his theories, such as that of non-perception (anupalabdhi), were strongly influenced at their starting point by Kumārila or others with similar views. The conclusion also calls for rethinking the common image of Mīmāṃsakas as holding a naive view of speech.

8Check your understanding

Click (tap) a question to reveal the answer.

Q1. In Mīmāṃsā, what is the single ultimate source of knowledge of dharma? The Vedic injunction (codanā). Human speech, Manu's teaching included, is at best a secondary source "based on the Veda."
Q2. According to Śabara's view ②, what kind of cognition arises from a human statement? Not "this object is thus," but "this person understands it thus." In other words, only the speaker's cognition is conveyed.
Q3. Which three kinds of failing speakers does Kumārila identify in the process of speaking? (1) One who has not seen the object (failure from object to cognition); (2) a liar (from cognition to intention); (3) a confused person (from intention to sentence).
Q4. Why is the Veda never false? It has no author (speaker), so no speaker's cognition intervenes and there is no room for defects. Its validity does not depend on a person (puṃnimitta).
Q5. How did Kumārila answer the question "Is human speech a pramāṇa?" He accepted it as a subcategory of pramāṇa. But it is valid within its own domain, the speaker's cognition. About the external object, its validity holds only once the source behind the speaker's cognition (perception or the Veda) is confirmed.
Q6. Where does the author locate the origin of Dharmakīrti's view that "speech indicates only the speaker's intention"? Not in Dignāga's apoha theory, but in Śabara's commentary and in Kumārila's development of it (or in Mīmāṃsakas holding similar views).
Think further: what happens if you apply this theory to news or social media posts? In Kumārila's terms, what a post tells you for certain is only that "this person thinks (or wants to say) so." Whether the content is true must be checked separately: did the poster actually see it (the source), are they honest, and did they express it accurately? You could see this as close to the idea of "fact-checking." (This is an analogy to aid understanding, not a claim made in the paper.)
